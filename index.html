How I got here

Three Interests, One Direction

I did not get to finance through the stock market. I got here through three things that had nothing to do with money and everything to do with how value actually gets built: slowly, from position, by people willing to do work nobody claps for.

I am a Finance and Accounting student at Florida State carrying a 3.9, and I have spent the last two years working the same thesis from three different seats — fixed income at Aevum Capital, municipal credit at United Capital, deal sourcing at Waterbrook Advisors, and equity research for FSU’s $11.5M Student Investment Fund. I am recruiting for investment banking now, as the training ground for the buy-side.

01

Jiu-Jitsu

Draft — your story goes here

Two to three sentences, in your voice: when you started, where you train, your belt, and one specific roll or competition you lost that changed how you train. The loss matters more than the wins. I will not write this for you.

Jiu-jitsu is the only place I have been taught leverage as a physical fact rather than a line in a model. A smaller person wins by being in the right position, and the same grip that finishes a match from on top gets you finished from underneath. Nothing about the leverage changed. Only the position did.

That is the whole of what I learned pricing credit at Aevum Capital, comparing investment grade against high yield to find where investors were actually paid for the risk they took, and what I learned at United Capital, checking whether an issuer’s project cash flows genuinely covered its debt service. Debt does not make a business good. It makes a position more permanent, in whichever direction it already points.

I want to be on the side of the table that has to live with that position for five years, not the side that hands it off at close.

02

Soccer

Draft — your story goes here

Two to three sentences: your UPSL club, your position, the season you made Miami Herald All-Conference Second Team, and whether you still play club at FSU. Then one match or stretch where you did everything right and still lost.

I played through the semi-professional level in the UPSL, and the thing nobody tells you about soccer is how little of it is scoring. Ninety minutes produces one goal, and that goal is the visible end of an hour of runs that went nowhere, a defensive shape held while the ball was somewhere else, and a hundred touches that only mattered because they were unremarkable.

That is also the argument for a roll-up, and it is the part people skip. When my team modeled Project Alpha, the returns did not come from a clever entry price — we held the same multiple going in and coming out. They came from centralizing back-office functions, putting BIM into the estimating process, and buying small contractors one at a time in a market too fragmented for anyone to have done it yet. None of that is a highlight. All of it is the goal.

Sponsors get paid for the hour of unremarkable work, and it is the only kind of compounding I actually trust.

03

Living Somewhere Else

Draft — confirm before this ships

The two paragraphs below are my proposed angle, not your words yet. Tell me what you actually noticed about how businesses operate in London across those eight weeks, and I will rewrite them around it. Also: what is the Côte d’Azur connection on your résumé — family, travel, study, or work?

Eight weeks in London was the first time I saw a services market that had already been consolidated. The trades, the facilities businesses, the contractors — categories that in Florida are still thousands of family-owned shops — had a handful of real players, and everyone in the industry knew their names.

That gap is not a curiosity. It is the entire lower-middle-market thesis, stated as a geography. At Waterbrook Advisors I screened more than 120 targets between $5M and $250M of enterprise value across fragmented industrials and services verticals, and the reason that list was so long is that the American version of those markets has not happened yet.

I would rather help build the consolidated version than write about it after someone else does.

Three interests, one habit: I keep being drawn to the part of a thing that does not show up in the result. That is not a reason to want the sell-side, where the work ends at close. It is a reason to want to own the outcome.

Project Alpha

Palmetto Partners — FSU Annual Mock Competition

A buy-side case on a mechanical, electrical, and plumbing contractor in Greenwood, Mississippi, generating roughly $168M of revenue across four divisions serving hospitals, schools, and industrial clients throughout the Sunbelt. We built it on three pillars:

  • An M&A platform in a market fragmented enough to actually consolidate
  • Margin expansion through BIM in estimating and centralized back-office functions
  • Sunbelt construction growth, data center demand, and aging infrastructure as tailwind

We held entry and exit at 7.5x EBITDA, so the returns had to come from operations and deleveraging rather than multiple expansion.

Credit · rates · structured products

Fixed Income & Municipal Credit

Aevum Capital · United Capital Financial Advisors

At Aevum I built models measuring how far bond prices move on a 100bp shift, and compared investment grade against high yield spreads over Treasuries to locate where investors were genuinely compensated for credit risk. I worked through prepayment behavior in mortgage-backed securities and seniority in asset-backed structures, and tracked how Federal Reserve policy moved yields across the curve.

At United Capital I evaluated infrastructure municipal bonds across general obligation and revenue issues, assessing issuer credit quality and whether project cash flows actually covered debt service, supporting fixed income allocation inside a $24B AUM portfolio.
