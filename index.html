How I got here

Four Interests, One Direction

My interest in investing did not start with the stock market. It started in four places that had nothing to do with money, and it took me a while to notice that each one was teaching me a different part of the same job.

I am a Finance and Accounting student at Florida State carrying a 3.9, and in two years I have worked that job from six seats. Fixed income at Aevum Capital, municipal credit at United Capital, capital markets and real estate special situations at LDR Partners, generalist M&A at Waterbrook Advisors, consumer staples equity research at Stanton Research Group, and media and telecom for the Undergraduate Investment Group, which runs FSU’s $11.5M Student Investment Fund.

I am recruiting for investment banking now, because it is the training ground. The goal after it is private equity, and the goal after that is doing it back home in South Florida.

What shaped me

The Interests Behind The Résumé

01

Goalkeeping

How to hold a downside

I captained Parkland Soccer Club in the UPSL, where most of the squad had grown up somewhere other than the United States. Goalkeeper is the hardest position from which to captain that team and the best one from which to learn how. I was the only player who could see the whole shape of the game, so the information was mine to move, and I had to move it forty yards in under a second to someone who did not necessarily share a first language with me. The instruction has to be shorter than the explanation.

It is also the only position on the field where the scoring is openly asymmetric. A striker can miss ten chances and get carried off for the eleventh. A keeper can be perfect for eighty-nine minutes and lose the match in the ninetieth. You are not credited for the saves, you are debited for the goals, and the job is to make the gap between those two numbers as boring as possible.

Which is the shape of a credit investment exactly. Lend well and you get par and a coupon. Lend badly and you get the whole loss. At Aevum I compared investment grade against high yield spreads over Treasuries, looking for where investors were genuinely paid for the risk they were taking. At United Capital I checked whether an issuer’s project cash flows actually covered debt service. Neither question is how good can this get. Both are what beats me, and from where.

First: the downside belongs to somebody, and I have never minded that it was mine.

02

Building Swish Tribune

How to build something that compounds

For nearly two years I ran marketing and analytics for Swish Tribune, a sports media page, and we took it past twenty thousand followers. What nobody tells you is how little of that comes from the post you are proud of. It comes from finding the two or three formats that reliably work and then running them on a schedule, on days when you would rather do something else, for months after it has stopped being interesting.

Analytics is what makes that bearable, because it keeps telling you that the thing you like is not the thing that works. I stopped arguing with it fairly early. That was probably the most useful part of the whole exercise: learning to let a number I did not like overrule an instinct I was attached to.

That is the half of a buyout that never fits on a slide. When my team underwrote Project Alpha, the returns did not come from buying cheaply and selling dearly. They came from centralizing back-office functions across a $168M contractor, putting BIM into the estimating process, and acquiring small competitors one at a time in a market too fragmented for anyone to have done it yet. None of that is a headline. All of it is the return.

Second: value is built by the boring repeatable thing, long after the interesting part is over.

03

Jiu-Jitsu

How leverage actually works

Jiu-jitsu is the only place I have been taught leverage as a physical fact rather than a line in a model. A smaller person wins from position. The identical grip that finishes a match from on top gets you finished from underneath, and nothing about the leverage has changed in between. Only the position has.

That is the argument of a leveraged buyout, and it is the part that gets skipped when people talk about returns. We held Project Alpha at the same 7.5x going in and coming out on purpose, precisely so the debt could not flatter us. Leverage did not make that contractor a good business. It made our position on it more permanent, in whichever direction it already pointed.

Third: leverage is not a lever you pull. It is a position you are already in, and I would rather be the one deciding which.

04

Leaving Home, Twice

How to see the arrangement you grew up inside

I grew up in Weston, Connecticut, and moved to Florida for high school. Moving as a teenager is a small and useful humiliation. Everything you assumed was simply how things are turns out to have been how things were, in that one place, among those particular people.

Then I kept leaving. Costa Rica, then Ecuador and a Quechua community in the Amazon, on trips that were mostly a matter of doing what you were told by people who knew things I did not. Later, eight weeks studying in London, and the Sahara in the photo above. None of it was a revelation. What it was, cumulatively, was practice at noticing that somebody chose this, and it could have been chosen differently.

A fragmented market is exactly that, and it never announces itself. It looks like a normal industry full of normal companies, right up until you have seen a version of it somebody already put together. At Waterbrook I screened more than 120 acquisition targets between $5M and $250M of enterprise value across fragmented industrials and services, and that list ran long because the American version of those markets has not been organized yet.

Fourth: the opportunity is usually a thing everyone is looking straight at and has stopped seeing.

Hold the downside. Build the thing that compounds. Structure the leverage. Find the market nobody has organized yet. Those are not four interests that happen to share a résumé. They are four quarters of one job, and there is exactly one seat where all four are the same job. An adviser does three of them brilliantly and hands the fourth to whoever has to live with the outcome. I would rather be the one living with it.

What I’d bring to Recalc

Community, Mentorship, and What I’m Missing

I was a mentee in FSU’s Investment Banking & Alternatives Academy before I was a mentor in it, and that order is the whole point. I am now one of a class of 16 in Noles on Wall Street, one of sixty in Access Distributed, one of ten selected into Florida State’s Markets Mentorship through the Global Markets Group, and a fellow in the Roark Capital Wall Street Club. Every one of those rooms exists because students built them. None came with the school.

Outside finance it is the same habit. Through Sigma Alpha Epsilon I coordinate fundraising for the Julia C. Before that it was service work in Costa Rica, Ecuador and the Amazon, and four years with Builders Beyond Borders back in Connecticut. I join things and then stay in them.

That is the honest version of my position: I have not lacked for effort or for programs. What I have not had is someone who actually ran deals sitting next to me while I build a model, telling me where my logic breaks.

Why Recalc specifically

  1. Modelling taught by people who used it

    Katie Fifer spent a decade in private equity at Audax Group and Paxion Capital, and Suzanne Corley came through J.P. Morgan. The LBO block is not a recorded course. It is seven live Tuesday sessions where someone who has underwritten real platforms can catch the assumption I got wrong while I am still typing it.

  2. Accounting as the foundation, not the afterthought

    I chose Finance and Accounting as a double major for the same reason the curriculum opens on accounting fundamentals before it touches an LBO. Most students want the model. The model is downstream of knowing what the statements are actually telling you.

  3. A partner list that includes credit

    TCW Private Credit sits alongside Audax, Charlesbank, Integrum, Silversmith, Spectrum Equity and TSG Consumer among the firms backing the program. Two of my six seats have been credit seats. That overlap is the most specific reason I am applying here rather than anywhere else.

  4. The Slack is a commitment, not a perk

    Consistent engagement and professional follow-through on introductions are stated requirements rather than bonuses. I would rather be held to that than left alone with a recording.

What I’d bring

  • A credit lens most sophomores do not have. Spreads, coverage and structure from two seats, before ever building an LBO.
  • Sourcing reps, not only modeling reps. 120+ lower-middle-market targets screened and mapped to decision-makers.
  • Something I built rather than joined. Two years and twenty thousand followers of learning that the unglamorous version is the one that works.
  • The habit of answering last year’s question. Mentee first, mentor second, in that order, and I intend to keep that order in your Slack.

The gap between my résumé and my ability is a modeling gap, and Recalc teaches exactly that, taught by people who have done it. I want the seat so that the route I have been building piece by piece — banking, then private equity, then doing it back home in South Florida — stops depending on me guessing at the part nobody has taught me yet. Give me the seat and I will be the member who shows up to all seven, answers in the Slack, and comes back next year to do it for somebody else.
